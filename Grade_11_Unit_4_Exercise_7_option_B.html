ASEAN YOUTH PROGRAMMES: LEARNING THROUGH COOPERATION

Exercise 7: Read the passage and choose the best answer to each question.

Regional youth programmes can offer much more than opportunities to travel or experience unfamiliar traditions. In many activities, students from different ASEAN countries work in mixed teams to explore shared concerns such as plastic waste, access to education or responsible use of technology. Rather than simply listening to presentations, participants may have to identify a problem, compare how it affects different communities and design a practical response.

Working across cultures, however, is not always straightforward. A behaviour that appears perfectly normal in one community may be interpreted differently in another. For example, remaining silent during a discussion might be seen as a sign of respect by one person but as a lack of interest by someone else. Participants therefore need to explain their intentions, ask questions when they are uncertain and adjust the way they communicate. Such interaction can challenge stereotypes, not because cultural differences disappear, but because students learn to handle them more constructively.

These programmes can also develop practical skills. A student team may need to divide responsibilities, meet deadlines, present ideas in English and revise a proposal after receiving feedback. In this context, leadership does not simply mean making decisions for everyone else. An effective student leader may instead coordinate different strengths, encourage quieter members to contribute and make sure that responsibility is shared. Even when a project does not produce the expected result, reflecting on what went wrong can turn the experience into a useful learning opportunity.

The value of a youth project also depends on how closely it responds to the community it is intended to serve. A short activity may attract attention but have little lasting effect if local needs have not been considered. Programmes are more likely to create meaningful outcomes when students communicate with local partners, understand existing problems and leave behind resources or plans that others can continue using.

Ultimately, the greatest value of regional youth programmes may lie not in the event itself but in what participants learn through sustained cooperation. By negotiating differences, solving problems and reflecting on their experiences, young people can develop skills that remain useful long after a particular programme has ended.
