WHEN FAILURE BECOMES PART OF INNOVATION

In workplaces that depend heavily on innovation, failure is often treated as something to be avoided. Employees may hesitate to propose unusual ideas because an unsuccessful project could damage their professional reputation. Yet some researchers and business leaders argue that this attitude can actually discourage innovation. When employees are expected to succeed every time, they may simply repeat familiar methods rather than experiment with approaches whose outcomes are uncertain.

This does not mean, however, that organisations should celebrate every unsuccessful project. A distinction needs to be made between avoidable mistakes and what some experts call “intelligent failures”. Avoidable mistakes may result from carelessness, inadequate preparation, or failure to follow established procedures. Intelligent failures, by contrast, occur when a carefully planned attempt to explore something genuinely new produces an unexpected result. Although such an attempt may not achieve its original objective, the information obtained from it can influence subsequent decisions.

For this reason, some organisations have begun encouraging employees to examine unsuccessful projects systematically rather than quietly abandoning them. Teams may discuss which assumptions proved incorrect, what warning signs were overlooked, and how similar problems could be prevented in the future. The purpose of such discussions is not to determine who deserves blame but to extract knowledge that can improve future performance. This approach is particularly valuable when the causes of failure are complex and cannot be attributed to a single individual.

Nevertheless, creating a workplace in which people can discuss mistakes openly is not straightforward. Managers who claim to welcome experimentation may unintentionally discourage it if employees are severely criticised whenever an idea fails. Similarly, an organisation that tolerates every mistake without examining its causes may encourage irresponsibility rather than innovation. The challenge, therefore, is to establish an environment where reasonable experimentation is supported while employees remain accountable for decisions they could reasonably have controlled.

This balance has become increasingly important as companies face rapidly changing technologies and consumer expectations. In such conditions, organisations cannot always rely on solutions that succeeded in the past. They need employees who are willing to test new possibilities but also capable of learning when those attempts do not work as intended. From this perspective, the ability to respond constructively to failure may be just as valuable as the ability to achieve immediate success.
