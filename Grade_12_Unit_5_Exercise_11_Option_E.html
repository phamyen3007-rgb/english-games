WHEN TECHNOLOGY SAVES TIME BUT CREATES MORE WORK

Digital technology was expected to make office work dramatically more efficient. Email replaced much written correspondence, shared documents made collaboration faster, and automated systems took over tasks that once required hours of manual effort. Yet many employees do not feel that technological progress has left them with more free time. Instead, some report having to respond to more messages, attend more virtual meetings, and manage a growing number of digital platforms. This apparent contradiction has led researchers to examine whether tools designed to save time may also generate new demands on workers.

One explanation involves what economists sometimes call the rebound effect. When technology makes an activity faster or cheaper, people do not necessarily use the resulting savings to do less. Instead, they may perform more of the activity. Email illustrates this clearly. Because sending a message requires little effort, employees can communicate with far more colleagues than was practical when correspondence was slower. Each individual message may take less time to produce, but the total volume of communication can increase so substantially that workers ultimately spend more, rather than less, time managing it. Efficiency at the level of an individual task, therefore, does not automatically translate into a reduction in overall workload.

Technology can also alter expectations about availability. When communication required employees to be physically present in an office, the boundary between working and non-working time was relatively visible. Smartphones and cloud-based platforms have weakened that boundary by making work accessible almost anywhere. Although such accessibility provides valuable flexibility, it may also create an implicit expectation that employees will respond outside conventional working hours. Importantly, managers do not always need to state this expectation directly. If colleagues regularly answer messages late at night, others may feel pressure to do the same.

Nor does introducing more technology necessarily improve coordination. Organisations sometimes adopt multiple digital tools to solve separate problems, only to discover that employees must repeatedly transfer information between systems or check several platforms for updates. The resulting fragmentation can interrupt concentration and increase the mental effort required to determine where information is located. In such cases, the difficulty does not arise because any individual tool is ineffective; rather, it emerges from the way numerous tools interact within the same working environment.

This does not mean that digital technology has failed to improve productivity. Many tasks can unquestionably be completed faster and more accurately than before. The more useful question is whether organisations actually convert those efficiencies into better working conditions and improved performance. Doing so may require them to examine not merely what each technology enables employees to do, but also what new expectations, behaviours, and processes emerge after it is introduced. The value of workplace technology, in other words, depends partly on how organisations respond to the changes that efficiency itself creates.
