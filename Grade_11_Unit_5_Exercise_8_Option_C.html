BEYOND A SINGLE SOLUTION TO CLIMATE CHANGE

✦Đọc bài bên trái rồi trả lời lần lượt 10 câu hỏi. Khi làm lại, thứ tự câu hỏi giữ nguyên và A/B/C/D được xáo trộn.

Climate change results from a combination of human activities, but the largest contribution comes from the release of greenhouse gases into the atmosphere. Burning fossil fuels for electricity, transport and industry releases large quantities of carbon dioxide, while agriculture and waste contribute other powerful greenhouse gases such as methane. Land-use change is another important factor. When forests are cleared, not only is stored carbon often released, but the Earth's capacity to remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere is also reduced. These processes strengthen the natural greenhouse effect and gradually alter the planet's energy balance.

The consequences extend far beyond a simple rise in average temperature. A warmer atmosphere can hold more moisture, which may contribute to heavier rainfall in some regions, while changes in circulation and evaporation can make drought more severe elsewhere. Rising sea levels increase the risk of coastal flooding, and prolonged heat can threaten human health, reduce crop yields and place additional pressure on energy systems. Natural ecosystems are also affected. Some species can move or adjust their behaviour, but others may not adapt rapidly enough, particularly when climate change occurs alongside habitat loss, pollution and other environmental pressures.

Addressing these risks requires two complementary strategies. The first, mitigation, aims to limit the extent of future climate change by reducing greenhouse gas emissions or increasing the amount of carbon removed from the atmosphere. Replacing fossil fuels with low-carbon energy, improving energy efficiency, protecting forests and developing cleaner transportation systems are common examples. However, even extremely ambitious emission reductions cannot immediately eliminate all climate impacts because greenhouse gases already released into the atmosphere continue to influence the climate.

[A] This makes adaptation equally necessary. [B] Adaptation involves adjusting human and natural systems to actual or expected climate effects. [C] For example, cities may expand green spaces to reduce extreme heat, farmers may adopt crops that tolerate drought, and coastal communities may improve flood defences. [D] Such measures do not stop climate change itself, but they can reduce the damage it causes.

Nevertheless, climate policies involve difficult choices. Resources are limited, and measures that appear effective at the national level may affect groups differently. For example, increasing energy prices may encourage efficiency but can create additional difficulties for low-income households unless appropriate support is provided. Similarly, communities that have contributed relatively little to global emissions may face particularly severe climate impacts. Effective climate policy therefore involves more than choosing technically efficient solutions; it also requires attention to who bears the costs, who receives the benefits and whether vulnerable groups have the resources to adapt.

Ultimately, neither mitigation nor adaptation is sufficient on its own. Strong mitigation reduces the scale of future changes, making adaptation more manageable, while effective adaptation protects people and ecosystems from impacts that cannot now be fully avoided. Treating the two approaches as competing alternatives may therefore weaken the overall response to climate change.
