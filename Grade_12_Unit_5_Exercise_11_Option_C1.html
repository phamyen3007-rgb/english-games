JOB SECURITY IN A CHANGING LABOUR MARKET

For much of the twentieth century, job security was commonly associated with staying with the same employer for many years. Workers who performed well could reasonably expect their positions to remain stable, particularly in large organisations. That assumption has become less reliable. Technological disruption, international competition, and changing consumer preferences can now transform entire industries within a relatively short period. As a result, even employees with permanent contracts may discover that the jobs for which they were originally hired have changed substantially or disappeared altogether.

This development has led some career experts to distinguish between job security and employment security. The former refers to the likelihood of keeping a particular position, whereas the latter concerns a person's ability to remain employable even if that position disappears. From this perspective, employees may achieve greater long-term security not by protecting their current job at all costs, but by continually developing skills that can be transferred to other roles or industries. Paradoxically, preparing to leave a job may sometimes be one of the most effective ways of protecting one's career.

Employers also have reasons to support this kind of development. Companies sometimes fear that providing employees with highly transferable skills will make it easier for them to leave. However, refusing to invest in training carries risks of its own. Workers whose skills become outdated may be less capable of responding to technological or organisational change. Furthermore, opportunities for professional development can increase employees' commitment to an organisation, even though those same opportunities make them more attractive to other employers.

This does not mean that workers should constantly change jobs simply to acquire new experience. Frequent movement between positions can prevent employees from developing the deeper expertise that comes from working on complex problems over an extended period. Nor is responsibility for employability entirely individual. Employers influence which training opportunities are available, while governments and educational institutions help determine whether workers can access affordable opportunities to retrain when industries decline.

Ultimately, employment security depends less on predicting exactly which occupations will survive than on being prepared for uncertainty. Workers cannot know precisely how their jobs will change over the next decade. They can, however, maintain their capacity to learn, adapt, and apply existing knowledge in unfamiliar situations. In an unpredictable labour market, security may therefore come not from avoiding change, but from becoming better equipped to respond to it.
