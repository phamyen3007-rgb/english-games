THE HIDDEN COST OF CONVENIENCE

Modern consumers have grown accustomed to services designed to eliminate waiting. Groceries can arrive within hours, entertainment is available instantly, and online retailers allow purchases to be completed with a few taps. Such conveniences are usually presented as straightforward improvements to everyday life. Yet researchers studying consumer behaviour have begun to question whether removing every minor inconvenience necessarily makes people better off. Their concern is not that convenience itself is harmful, but that its benefits may be accompanied by consequences that are easy to overlook.

One such consequence involves decision-making. In traditional stores, purchasing something requires several steps: travelling to the shop, locating the product, and physically paying for it. Online platforms can reduce this process to seconds. This efficiency is valuable when consumers know exactly what they need, but it can also weaken the natural pauses during which people reconsider unnecessary purchases. Features such as one-click ordering and automatically stored payment information further reduce the effort required to spend money. By removing what economists sometimes call “friction”, digital services may make desirable actions easier while simultaneously making impulsive ones harder to resist.

Convenience may also affect people's expectations. Once consumers become accustomed to receiving products or services immediately, even modest delays can begin to feel unacceptable. Businesses consequently face pressure to shorten delivery times further, sometimes at considerable financial and environmental cost. Maintaining extremely rapid delivery, for example, may require companies to operate partially filled vehicles rather than wait until more efficient delivery routes can be organised. Thus, a service that saves several minutes for an individual consumer may generate costs that are distributed across businesses, workers, and the environment.

There is also evidence that convenience can change how people value what they receive. Psychologists have long observed that effort can influence perceived value: people sometimes appreciate an outcome more when achieving it requires meaningful involvement. This does not imply that difficulty is inherently beneficial or that inefficient services should be deliberately preserved. Rather, it suggests that eliminating effort indiscriminately may remove experiences that contribute to satisfaction, learning, or a sense of achievement. The important distinction, therefore, is between pointless obstacles and productive effort.

None of this suggests that society should reject convenient technologies. Their advantages, particularly for people with limited time, reduced mobility, or restricted access to physical services, can be substantial. The broader lesson is that convenience should be evaluated not simply by asking how much effort it eliminates, but by considering what else changes when that effort disappears. A service that saves time may still alter spending habits, expectations, environmental costs, or even the value people attach to an experience. Convenience, in other words, is best understood as a trade-off rather than an unquestionable good.
