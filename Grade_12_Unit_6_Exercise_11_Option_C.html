WHEN SEEING IS NO LONGER BELIEVING

[I] Until recently, producing a convincing fake video or voice recording required specialist knowledge, considerable computing power, and a large amount of source material. [II] Today, commercially available generative AI tools can reproduce a person's face or voice from a relatively small number of photographs or short recordings. [III] As a result, deception that once demanded substantial technical resources can now be attempted by relatively inexperienced criminals on a much larger scale. [IV] The growing accessibility of synthetic-media technology has therefore changed not only how convincing online fraud can be, but also how quickly and cheaply it can be carried out.

This change has transformed several familiar forms of fraud. Criminals may impersonate company executives during video meetings, imitate relatives during emergency phone calls, or use digitally reproduced celebrities to promote fraudulent investments. What makes these schemes particularly effective is that they exploit people's existing expectations. Employees are accustomed to following instructions from senior managers, family members are likely to react emotionally to urgent requests, and consumers may associate familiar public figures with credibility. In each case, the technology does not create trust from nothing; rather, it borrows trust that already exists and redirects it towards a fraudulent purpose. Consequently, even people who are generally cautious online may respond before noticing that something is wrong.

Detecting manipulated content is becoming increasingly difficult because the technology evolves in response to the methods used to expose it. Early deepfakes sometimes contained obvious visual errors, including unnatural blinking, poor lip synchronization, or inconsistencies in lighting. Once researchers publicized these weaknesses, however, developers learned to reduce them. Detection software now examines subtle patterns in pixels, speech, and movement that humans may overlook, but these systems are not infallible. New forms of manipulation can appear before detection tools have been updated to recognize them. This creates an uneven contest in which defenders must prepare for many possible forms of deception, while attackers may succeed by exploiting only one overlooked weakness.

For this reason, cybersecurity specialists increasingly emphasize verification rather than relying solely on the ability to recognize a fake. If someone unexpectedly requests money or confidential information, users are advised to contact that person through a separate, previously trusted channel. Organisations can also require more than one employee to approve unusual financial transfers and establish code words or other internal procedures for sensitive requests. People should also limit the amount of personal material they make publicly available, since photographs, videos, job titles, family relationships, and even travel plans can help criminals create more convincing impersonations. Most importantly, surprising or emotionally charged content should be corroborated through independent sources before people act on it or share it.

Deepfake technology will probably continue to improve, and no single defensive method is likely to remain effective forever. This does not mean that users are helpless. Instead, the growing sophistication of synthetic media makes careful verification more important than instinctive trust. In an environment where a familiar face or voice can be artificially reproduced, security increasingly depends on confirming identity through evidence that is independent of the suspicious message itself.
