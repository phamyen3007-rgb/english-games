CLIMATE RESILIENCE DEPENDS ON SHARED RESPONSIBILITY

✦Đọc bài bên trái rồi trả lời lần lượt 8 câu hỏi. Khi làm lại, thứ tự câu hỏi giữ nguyên và A/B/C/D được xáo trộn.

As the effects of climate change become increasingly visible, governments are under growing pressure not only to reduce greenhouse gas emissions but also to prepare communities for changes that can no longer be completely avoided. Rising temperatures are contributing to more intense heatwaves, shifting rainfall patterns and higher risks of flooding in many regions. However, exposure to these hazards does not automatically result in the same level of damage everywhere. Communities with reliable infrastructure, effective public services and sufficient financial resources are generally better able to anticipate, withstand and recover from climate-related events.

For this reason, climate resilience has become an important part of international climate policy. Resilience refers to the capacity of societies to cope with climate impacts while continuing to function and, where possible, adapting to future conditions. Building resilience may involve improving drainage systems, developing heat-warning programmes, protecting coastal areas or changing agricultural practices. Yet such measures can be expensive, particularly for countries that have contributed relatively little to historical greenhouse gas emissions but face serious climate risks.

International cooperation can help address this imbalance. Wealthier countries and international institutions may provide finance, technical expertise and access to climate-related technologies. Nevertheless, simply transferring money or technology does not guarantee successful adaptation. Solutions developed in one country may be unsuitable elsewhere because environmental conditions, local economies and community priorities differ. Effective programmes therefore often combine scientific knowledge with the experience of local people who understand the challenges facing their own communities.

At the same time, adaptation should not be treated as an alternative to reducing emissions. If global temperatures continue to rise rapidly, some climate impacts may eventually become too severe or costly to manage effectively. Adaptation can reduce vulnerability, but emission reductions are necessary to limit the scale of future risks. A lasting response to climate change therefore requires both approaches: international cooperation must support communities in dealing with present impacts while countries continue working to prevent even greater changes in the future.
