ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE: PRODUCTIVITY, RISK, AND HUMAN RESPONSIBILITY

Artificial intelligence is becoming deeply embedded in modern life. It recommends what people watch, assists doctors in interpreting medical images, supports businesses in analysing large amounts of data, and increasingly performs tasks that once required human workers. Supporters argue that AI can raise productivity and improve decision-making, while critics warn that its rapid adoption may create social and economic difficulties. The important question, therefore, is not simply whether AI is beneficial or harmful, but under what conditions its advantages can be realised without allowing its risks to become unmanageable.

One major advantage of AI is its ability to process information rapidly and consistently. In workplaces, intelligent systems can take over repetitive administrative duties, allowing employees to devote more attention to activities requiring judgment, creativity, or interpersonal communication. AI can also identify patterns in complex data that humans might overlook. In healthcare, for instance, some systems can examine medical scans and highlight abnormalities for doctors to investigate further. This does not mean that AI should make medical decisions independently; rather, it can function as an additional source of evidence for trained professionals. Similarly, manufacturers can use AI to detect equipment problems before machines fail, reducing both disruption and maintenance costs.

Despite these benefits, widespread automation may also displace some workers, particularly those employed in occupations dominated by predictable and repetitive tasks. However, the effect on employment is unlikely to be uniform. While certain jobs may disappear or shrink, others may be redesigned, and entirely new roles may emerge. The challenge lies in whether workers can acquire the skills needed to move into these changing occupations. Another concern is the growing capacity of generative AI to produce convincing but inaccurate material. Artificially generated images, audio recordings, and written reports can make misinformation more difficult to recognise, particularly when users accept online content without checking its source. The greater the realism of synthetic content, the less reliable appearance alone becomes as evidence of authenticity.

These concerns do not necessarily justify rejecting AI altogether. Instead, governments, schools, and businesses may need to establish safeguards that encourage useful innovation while limiting predictable harm. Workers whose jobs are changing may require opportunities for retraining, while students need to learn how to evaluate AI-generated information critically. Organisations should also remain accountable for important decisions made with the assistance of automated systems. Ultimately, AI is most likely to benefit society when it complements human expertise rather than replacing human responsibility. Its future impact will depend not only on what the technology can do, but also on how carefully people decide to use it.
